The problem

Your ridge is only half used.

Your hill farm sits on one of the best wind resources in Europe, and you have already paid for the hardest parts: the permit, the roads, the land agreements and the grid connection. Yet the turbines use only the upper part of the wind. The layer near the crest, below the blade tips, produces nothing.

Repowering makes the gap bigger. Many low turbines are replaced by fewer, taller ones, so the lower layer is cleared and left empty, while the grid connection you fought for may end up underused.

Meanwhile, new capacity is getting harder to add:

New sites are scarce.

Good wind sites are rare, flat land is taken by homes and farming, and ever-larger turbines are harder to move up a hill.

Permits get tougher.

Communities object to infrasound and bird deaths. Wildlife conditions in new permits mean curtailment: bat curtailment costs 0.3-1% of annual output in published studies, and German law requires operators to accept bird-protection curtailment of up to 8% of annual yield.3,4

Wildlife incidents cost real money.

ESI Energy (NextEra), US, 2022: $1.86M fine plus $6.21M restitution for eagle deaths at 50 facilities.1 Aumelas (EDF Renouvelables), France, 2025: €5M in fines (half suspended) and 31 turbines stopped for 4 months.2

Legacy turbines don't last.

About 20 years, then replacement, with fibreglass blades that are almost impossible to recycle.

Sources: 1 US Department of Justice 2022 · 2 franceinfo 2025 · 3 Arnett et al. 2011 · 4 BNatSchG §45b

The solution and how it works

WindNext fills the lower layer of your wind farm.

WindNext is a modular multi-rotor wind turbine designed for hills. Instead of one big rotor on a tall tower, it uses rows of almost-touching 17.4 m rotors, fixed in place along the ridge crest, in two stacked rows. The rows reach about 43.7 m, so they fit in the clear space beneath the blades of large turbines.

  1. 1

    Two rows along the crest.

    Rotors of 17.4 m, about 135 kW each, with hubs at 18 m and 35 m. The rows follow the ridge line.

  2. 2

    Fixed, facing the ridge wind.

    The rows don't yaw. Cross-ridge wind is caught from both sides of the crest, so a single row works whichever way the prevailing wind blows across the ridge.

  3. 3

    Three effects, one module.

    The shroud is designed to enhance the jet effect. Rotors close together increase the yield of each one. Wind arriving at a ±30° angle increases yield by up to 35%.

  4. 4

    Built on site from small parts.

    95% of each module is made of only four parts: hoop modules, crossbar holders, shroud envelope parts and blades. The biggest segment is 1.5 × 11.3 × 2.2 m and weighs 2.3 tonnes, so it travels on smaller trucks and goes up with smaller cranes on your existing roads.

The repowering use case

Repowering is the moment to plan WindNext in.

When you repower, you rewrite the layout of your site anyway. Taller hubs and longer blades lift the rotors away from the crest and clear the lower layer. That is exactly where WindNext works.

Design both layers in one go: the big turbines above, WindNext rows below, in one layout, one permit process and one connection plan. You keep the roads, the land agreements and the grid connection, and you add MWh that the new layout would otherwise leave on the table.

From your site to a pilot, in three steps:

STEP 1

Clearance check.

We check geometrically that your existing or planned blades leave room for WindNext rows at the base of the farm.

STEP 2

Repowering fit.

We place WindNext rows into your repowering layout and check grid headroom and the lease of the crest strip.

STEP 3

Pilot-site study.

A study on one of your sites leads to a first WindNext installation. First projects in 2027.

Also works without repowering: where today's blades already leave enough clearance, WindNext rows can be added to an operating farm. One of our first pilot projects is an expansion of an existing wind farm.

Birds, bats and noise

Quiet for neighbours.

Birds and bats

Legacy rotors are nearly invisible and turn with the wind, so birds have no constant obstacle to learn. WindNext rows are fixed in place with an obvious shroud, a constant obstacle that birds and bats see and avoid. WindNext turbines are certified safe for birds and bats.

Certified safe for birds and bats. Zero infrasound.

Infrasound

In legacy turbines, high-speed blades pass a large tower and create strong pressure waves: infrasound that can cause chronic noise stress in neighbours. WindNext has no tower in front of or behind the blades. Its rotors turn at 12 to 60 RPM, so they cannot create sound below 20 Hz. Zero infrasound, by design.

Why it matters to you

Wildlife and noise are where permits stall and where curtailment and fines come from. A low, fixed, visible turbine without infrasound gives your EIA team and the community a different conversation.

Monitoring commitment

Our pilots include bird and bat monitoring with DOPPS (BirdLife Slovenia) and local BirdLife partners, plus acoustic and infrasound measurements, and we will publish the results.

Sustainability

Built once. Built for 60 years. Fully recyclable.

Galvanised steelAircraft-grade aluminiumFully recyclable

A legacy turbine is replaced after about 20 years, and its fibreglass blades usually end up in landfill or an incinerator. WindNext is designed to last up to 60 years with scheduled maintenance: one WindNext installation covers the life of three generations of legacy turbines.

All parts except the generator and electrical equipment are galvanised steel and aircraft-grade aluminium. The blades are fully recyclable too. At the end of its service life, the module goes back into the material cycle.

Small parts mean smaller foundations, less soil work and fewer heavy transports up the hill, so the build itself leaves a lighter footprint on the ridge.

FAQ

Operator questions

Can't find your question? Contact Jan directly.

Will WindNext interfere with my existing turbines?

Every site gets a geometric clearance check first, so the rows stay in the clear space beneath your blades. The pilot measures wake and turbulence interaction with your existing rotors using lidar.

How much energy will it add?

It depends on your ridge, its wind and the length of crest available. Estimated, average case: about 15,500 MWh a year per km of ridge (typical range 4,000-26,000), with about 15 MW installed per km (about 115 rotors). By mean wind at 35 m hub: 6 m/s about 11,600, 7 m/s about 16,500, 8 m/s about 21,500 MWh/year per km. At existing farms the usable crest is often only a few hundred metres, so see the wind farm map for per-farm figures. Your site study gives an indicative yield range; the pilot verifies the power curve to IEC 61400-12-1.

The rows are fixed. What if the wind changes direction?

WindNext is designed for ridges where most of the wind blows across the crest. The rows take wind from both sides, and wind arriving at a ±30° angle increases yield by up to 35%. Our screening found 772 of 2,251 hill and ridge wind farms in the EU-27 and UK with this cross-ridge wind.

Will I need a new permit or EIA?

That depends on the country and on your existing permit. The best route is to design WindNext into your repowering permit. Smaller foundations and less soil work help planning permission move faster. [to confirm: country-specific permitting guidance for Spain, Greece, Croatia and Slovenia]

Can I use my existing grid connection?

Yes, that is the idea: more MWh through the connection you already have. We check the headroom in the site study. Where the connection is full, WindNext can be paired with storage or curtailment scheduling.

Is it certified? Is it bankable?

Type certification is under way and the type certificate is planned for 2028, together with first deliveries. Pilots include independent power-curve measurement. WindNext turbines are certified safe for birds and bats.

When can I have it on my site?

First projects in 2027: site studies and pilot agreements. First deliveries in 2028.

What about birds, bats and noise?

WindNext rows are fixed and visible, certified safe for birds and bats, and produce no infrasound: there is no tower in front of or behind the blades, and the rotors turn at 12-60 RPM. Pilots include bird, bat and infrasound monitoring.

How hard is it to install on a hill?

Modules are assembled on site from small components. The biggest segment is 1.5 × 11.3 × 2.2 m and weighs 2.3 tonnes, so smaller trucks and cranes do the job on existing access roads.

What about extreme weather and icing?

WindNext is designed to withstand winds over 300 km/h. Icing on crests is part of the pilot measurement plan.

What does it cost?
